1. What FEDzk is

FEDzk (Federated Learning + Zero-Knowledge) is a framework in which participants collaboratively train a model under Federated Averaging (or similar aggregation), but each client must attach a cryptographic proof that its submitted update satisfies a publicly agreed integrity policy encoded as an arithmetic circuit—without requiring the coordinator to re-run local training or see the client’s private dataset.

One-sentence product concept: “Only updates that can prove they obey the rules get into the global model.”

FEDzk is simultaneously:

  • A systems framework (train → quantize → prove → verify → aggregate → measure).
  • A research platform (configs, transcripts, attack suites, baselines).
  • A cryptographic application of Groth16 zkSNARKs to FL update integrity.

2. Problem we are solving

2.1 Federated learning’s trust gap

Classical FL (McMahan et al.) keeps raw data on clients and ships model updates to a coordinator. That improves data locality, but the coordinator still trusts clients to send valid updates. A malicious client can send unbounded, sparse-but-poisonous, or garbage updates and damage the global model (Byzantine / model poisoning).

2.2 Verification without re-training

The coordinator cannot simply “check the training” without either (a) seeing private data or (b) trusting the client’s word. We need a check that is:

  • Locally enforceable as a predicate on the update (and related public parameters),
  • Efficient to verify relative to training,
  • Binding so the client cannot claim one update and submit another,
  • Optionally zero-knowledge so verification does not force extra leakage beyond the update itself.

2.3 Open infrastructure gap

Academic ZK-FL systems often lack a complete, honest, reproducible open-source pipeline. FEDzk’s contribution concept includes making that pipeline real enough to cite and extend.

5. The FEDzk round (conceptual loop)

1. Setup (once per circuit version): compile Circom → R1CS/wasm; trusted setup → pk, vk
2. Coordinator announces: round_id, w^t, circuit_id, scale_bits, bounds B,m, N
3. Client: local train on private D_k → raw update g
4. Client: quantize g → ĝ with scale s   [WHY: SNARK fields are integers]
5. Client: if dim(ĝ) > N, chunk + commit  [WHY: circuits are fixed-width]
6. Client: prove π for “ĝ (chunks) satisfy L and bind to commitment/round”
7. Client → Coordinator: (ĝ or refs, π, public signals, meta)
8. Coordinator: structural validate → Verify(vk, x, π) → accept or reject
9. On enough accepts: FedAvg → w^{t+1}; emit metrics/transcript events
10. Clients pull w^{t+1}; next round

How this differs from plain FL: step 6–8 are mandatory gates. Aggregation never sees unverified junk (unless misconfigured—which QUALITY forbids).

6. What the proof asserts

Informally, a successful verify means:

∃ witness w s.t. Circuit(w, x) = 1
where x includes public policy parameters and binding data,
and w includes the quantized update (and auxiliaries).

For the secure-update family, the intended English statement is:

  • “I know a quantized vector ĝ of length N (or chunks of one committed vector),
  • whose squared L2 norm is ≤ B,
  • with at least m nonzero entries (if enabled),
  • consistent with the public commitment / round_id binding.”

It does not assert: “my local loss decreased,” “my data is IID,” “I am not colluding,” or “ĝ is optimal.”

Why ZK specifically: the witness may include intermediate signals we do not want to publish, and we want a stable verify interface that does not expand as circuits grow. Even when ĝ itself is delivered to the coordinator for FedAvg (common in v1), the proof still provides succinct verifiable integrity and a path to future designs where more stays private (e.g. proof of properties + secure aggregation).

8. Why client-side proofs (design positioning)

Literature such as zkFL emphasizes proving that the aggregator correctly averaged client models. FEDzk’s primary v1 concept is the dual problem: proving that each client update meets policy before it is allowed into the average.

  • Why this first: poisoning enters through clients; filtering bad Δ is immediately useful in classical FL topologies.
  • Why not only this forever: a malicious aggregator can still drop/replace updates—cite and optionally add aggregator ZK later.
  • How we design for complementarity: keep proof schema and transcript events composable with future aggregator proofs.

9. Why quantization exists

Circom / Groth16 operate over a finite field. Machine learning gradients are floating-point. Without a defined encode/decode:

  • Witness generation fails (BigInt conversion errors), or
  • Developers invent ad-hoc casts that break soundness or reproducibility.

How we design it: first-class GradientQuantizer with scale_bits, clip bounds, and metadata in the proof envelope so verifiers and papers know the numeric regime.

Why it must be on the prove path: optional quantize is how the current demo breaks on real tensors. Conceptually, quantization is part of the statement’s encoding, not a convenience helper.

10. Why circuits / constraint languages

A circuit is how we turn an English policy (“norm at most B”) into something a SNARK can prove. We use Circom in v1 because:

  • It compiles to R1CS understood by snarkjs / arkworks-circom stacks,
  • The ecosystem (circomlib gadgets) matches bound/comparison patterns we need,
  • Migrating to Noir/Halo2 is a proving-system redesign (Strategy D), not a refactor.

Why fixed width N: R1CS templates are statically sized. Real models need chunking + commitments rather than pretending one circuit holds 10M floats.

11. Roles: client, prove service, coordinator

Client

  • Concept: data trustee + prover (or prove-client).
  • Why local train stays here: privacy of D_k; FL definition.
  • Why prove may be remote: proving is CPU-heavy; thin clients need a prove service—but policy must still bind to their update.

Prove service (“MPC server” in current code)

  • Concept: outsourced witness+prove worker, not multiparty computing in the cryptographic MPC sense (naming legacy).
  • Why it exists: operational separation of heavy proving; shared artifact mounts; scaling workers.
  • How we design trust: treat it as trusted for availability/performance unless we add oblivious proving later; still cannot accept unverified updates at coordinator.

Coordinator

  • Concept: policy enforcer + aggregator + round clock.
  • Why verify-before-aggregate: integrity gate; the entire product collapses if skipped.
  • Why single coordinator API: dual schemas (legacy aggregator vs logic API) destroy the concept of one protocol.

14. Threat model concepts

  • Malicious client: crafts ĝ ∉ L or forged π → must fail verify (soundness).
  • Honest-but-curious coordinator: follows protocol; may try to infer extra from ĝ/π; ZK limits witness leakage; ĝ still revealed in v1 FedAvg.
  • Network adversary: TLS + auth; replay detected via proof_hash/round_id.
  • Setup adversary: if toxic waste retained, soundness can break—operational concept, not just crypto trivia.
  • Out of scope v1: malicious aggregator rewriting averages; side channels in prove service.

15. Conceptual limitations (do not paper over)

  • In-bound adversarial updates still possible if they satisfy L.
  • Quantization/chunking can change learning dynamics.
  • Prove cost may dominate tiny local epochs—report honestly.
  • Circuit bugs are security bugs; tests + review required.
  • Current legacy code (n=4, unwired quantize) does not instantiate these concepts yet—agents build toward them.

16. Mental models & analogies

  • Airport liquids rule: you can bring a bottle (update) but it must pass a size rule (circuit); ZK is a scanner that checks the rule without tasting your drink (extra witness detail).
  • Type system at the edge: L is a type; the proof is a certificate that ĝ typechecks before the aggregator’s “compiler” (FedAvg) runs.
  • CI for model updates: coordinator is a merge gate; π is the required green check.
